RACE WEEKEND

Three days, three jobs

A Grand Prix weekend is built to get a car from an unknown setup on Friday to its fastest, most reliable version by Sunday afternoon.

FRIDAY — SATURDAY AM

Practice

Three sessions where teams test tyre choices, fuel loads, and car balance. Times don't count for anything except information.

3 × 60min
SATURDAY

Qualifying

A knockout format across three stages sets Sunday's starting grid — the slowest cars are eliminated after each stage.

Q1 / Q2 / Q3
SUNDAY

The Race

A fixed distance (roughly 305km) run over as many laps as that takes, with pit stops for fresh tyres built into the strategy.

~90–120min
THE CAR

What's actually under that bodywork

01

Front and rear wings

Shaped like upside-down aircraft wings, they push the car down onto the track instead of lifting it up.

02

The monocoque

A carbon-fibre survival cell around the driver, built to absorb enormous impact forces in a crash.

03

Hybrid power unit

A turbocharged engine paired with energy-recovery systems that harvest braking and exhaust heat for extra power.

04

18-inch slick tyres

No tread pattern in the dry — maximum rubber touching the track for grip, with separate wet-weather tyres for rain.

PIT STOPS

Twenty people, two seconds

A modern pit stop is one of the most rehearsed few seconds in sport — every crew member has exactly one job.

0.0s

Car arrives at its marked box

The driver brakes to a precise stop within centimetres of the pit crew's position.

0.3s

Jacks lift front and rear

Two jack operators raise the car off the ground almost simultaneously.

0.8s

Wheels off, wheels on

Three-person crews per corner remove one wheel nut, pull the old tyre, and fit the new one.

~2.2s

Car is released

The lollipop or light system signals it's safe to rejoin — a fraction of a second early or late can cost a position.

COCKPIT

A steering wheel that does more than steer

The steering wheel is closer to a cockpit control panel than a road car's. Drivers adjust brake balance, engine mapping, differential settings, and radio channels — all without taking their hands off it.

A small display in the centre shows lap times, tyre temperatures, fuel remaining, and warnings, all read at speeds well over 300km/h.

CIRCUITS

Not every track is built the same way

Two broad categories

PERMANENT CIRCUIT

Purpose-built tracks like Monza, shown here, used year-round for racing and testing, with run-off areas designed for high-speed corners.

STREET CIRCUIT

Public roads closed off for the weekend, lined by walls instead of gravel — tighter, less forgiving, and often slower on average.

HYBRID CIRCUIT

A mix of dedicated racing sections and public roads, combining higher speeds with some street-circuit-style tight corners.

FLAGS & SIGNALS

How marshals talk to drivers at 300km/h

Green flag

Track is clear — session or race is starting, or a hazard has just been cleared.

Yellow flag

Danger ahead — no overtaking in that sector until the hazard is passed.

Red flag

Session stopped entirely, usually for a serious incident or unsafe conditions.

Chequered flag

The session or race has finished — shown to every car as it crosses the line.

Blue flag

Shown to a car about to be lapped, asking it to let the faster car through.

Yellow and red stripes

Warns of a slippery track surface, often oil or debris rather than a stopped car.

MILESTONES

A short history of the sport

1950

The first World Championship season

The inaugural Formula One World Championship season began at Silverstone in the United Kingdom.

1977

Turbocharged engines arrive

Renault introduced the first turbocharged engine in the modern era, changing engine design for the following decade.

1994

A wave of major safety reforms

Following serious accidents that season, the sport introduced sweeping changes to car design and circuit safety standards.

2014

The hybrid power unit era begins

New regulations introduced turbo-hybrid engines with energy recovery systems, reshaping both performance and strategy.

2022

A return to ground-effect aerodynamics

Major rule changes brought back underbody aerodynamics last common in the 1970s and 80s, aimed at improving close racing.
